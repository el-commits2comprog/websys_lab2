I Can't Control the Tide and I'm Learning to Be Okay With That.

February 2026 · Reading · 10 min read

Emotions Waves Personal Growth
February 23, 2026
Emotions
10 minutes

I have always felt things deeply. Not quietly, not in small doses — but fully, loudly, all the way through. A song can undo me. A conversation can sit heavy in my chest for days. Joy, when it comes, fills every corner of the room. And grief, when it arrives, arrives like weather.

For a long time, I treated this as a flaw. Something to apologize for, to explain, to keep carefully tucked away so it wouldn't spill onto anyone else. I would bury things before they had the chance to surface. I became very good at intellectualizing my feelings — naming them, analyzing them, turning them into something I could handle at arm's length — without ever actually letting myself feel them.

What I didn't understand then was that burying something doesn't make it disappear. It just means you meet it later, when you're less prepared.

The Tide Doesn't Ask for Permission

I think about emotions the way I think about the ocean. You can observe the tide from a distance and understand exactly how it works — the gravitational pull, the predictable rhythm of high and low. You can be entirely rational about it. But the moment you're standing at the shoreline and a wave crashes into you, all that understanding doesn't stop the water.

That is what it feels like to have big emotions. You can know, intellectually, that you are safe. You can know that this feeling will pass, that it is not permanent, that you have survived every version of it before. And still — the wave comes, and it is cold, and it is fast, and it takes your breath for a moment.

The goal, I've learned, is not to stop the wave. You cannot negotiate with the tide. The goal is to learn how to stand in the water without being swept away.

What We Lose When We Stop Feeling

There is a version of emotional management that looks like control but is actually just numbness. The walls go up. The feelings get filed away. You become very efficient, very composed, and quietly hollow. I have been that version of myself. I don't recommend it.

Because here is the thing about shutting off the painful feelings — you can't selectively numb. When you bury the grief, you bury the joy along with it. When you stop letting yourself feel the hard things, the beautiful things start to lose their edges too. Everything flattens. You are no longer in pain, but you are no longer really present either.

I would rather feel everything than nothing at all. I would rather be someone who cries at the right song, who feels the weight of a goodbye, who lets happiness in so fully it becomes something she can actually remember. The depth of what I feel is not a defect. It is the same capacity that lets me love well, notice things other people miss, and be genuinely present with the people I care about.

I used to curse myself for feeling everything so deeply. Now I'm learning that the depth is not the problem — it's the part of me worth keeping.

Befriending the Wave

The shift I am slowly making is from fighting my emotions to befriending them. This sounds simple. It is not.

Befriending an emotion means sitting with it instead of running from it. It means asking — what are you here to tell me? — instead of immediately trying to make it stop. Anger is rarely just anger; underneath it there is usually something that feels like hurt or fear or a boundary that got crossed. Sadness is not weakness; it is often just love with nowhere to go. Anxiety, at its core, is a nervous system trying very hard to keep you safe.

When I stop treating my emotions as enemies to be defeated and start treating them as messengers, they stop feeling so overwhelming. They are not attacking me. They are trying to communicate. The moment I genuinely listen — not analyze, not intellectualize, but actually feel — is the moment the grip begins to loosen.

Learning to Be Steady in the Water

Regulating your nervous system is not the same as suppressing your feelings. It is learning to stay present with what you feel without being consumed by it. It is the difference between being in the ocean and being pulled under by it.

I am still learning this. Some days the tide is low and everything is manageable and I feel like I have finally figured it out. Other days a wave comes out of nowhere and I am right back at the beginning — breathless, overwhelmed, wondering how I am still this person. But I am gentler about it now than I used to be. I don't curse myself for feeling anymore. I don't see the wave as proof that something is wrong with me. I see it as proof that I am alive, and paying attention, and still very much in the process of becoming.

The tide is not something I will ever fully control. But I am learning to trust that I can stand in it. That it will move through me and not destroy me. That the same water that knocks me sideways is also the water I have been swimming in my entire life — and I have not drowned yet.

A Note to Anyone Who Feels Too Much

If you are someone who has ever been told you are too sensitive, too emotional, too much — I want you to know that I understand, and I don't think it's true. Your emotions are not a burden. They are not a character flaw dressed up as a feeling. They are information, and they are valid, and they belong to you.

You don't have to perform calm you don't feel. You don't have to shrink the parts of you that feel loudly. You just have to learn, slowly and imperfectly, to be a steady enough shore that the waves can come in without destroying everything. That is the work. Not to feel less — but to feel safely. To befriend the tide instead of building walls against it.

I'm still learning. But I'd rather be learning this than have stopped feeling altogether.
